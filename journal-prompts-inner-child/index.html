Inner child journal prompts

Inner child journal prompts are gentle questions that help you reconnect with the child you used to be: what you loved, what you needed, and what you learned. Go slowly, start with happy memories, and stop if something feels too heavy. Below are 64 prompts for remembering, play, healing old messages and caring for yourself now.

What inner child journaling is

Your inner child is a way of describing the feelings, needs and memories from childhood that you still carry. When you react to something with feelings that seem bigger than the moment, it is often an old feeling showing up.

Inner child journaling means writing to, about and sometimes from that younger part of you. It can bring back joy you forgot about. It can also help you give yourself the kindness you needed back then.

Inner child work can bring up painful memories. Go at your own pace and skip any prompt that feels like too much. Journaling is not a replacement for professional care. If childhood memories feel overwhelming, a licensed therapist can help. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency services. In the US, call or text 988.

How to start inner child journaling safely

  1. Start with good memoriesBegin with the remembering and play prompts. They help you feel connected before you touch anything hard.
  2. Find a calm momentWrite when you have time and privacy, not when you are rushed or already upset.
  3. Look at an old photoA picture of yourself as a child can make the writing feel real and tender.
  4. Write with kindnessTalk to your younger self like you would talk to any small child: gently and without blame.
  5. Close softlyEnd each session with something comforting, like a walk, a warm drink or a favorite song.

Inner child prompts to remember who you were

  1. What did my bedroom look like when I was seven? Describe one corner of it.
  2. What was my favorite thing to wear as a child?
  3. What did I want to be when I grew up, and why?
  4. Who was my favorite person to be around as a kid?
  5. What was a typical Saturday morning like when I was small?
  6. What cartoon, book or song did I love most?
  7. What did I collect or keep in a special box?
  8. Which smell takes me straight back to childhood?
  9. Where did I go when I wanted to be alone?
  10. What was I really good at as a kid that no one gave grades for?
  11. Look at an old photo of myself. What do I notice in my face?

Prompts about joy and play

  1. What games did I make up as a child?
  2. What made me laugh so hard I could not stop as a kid?
  3. What did I do with a whole summer day?
  4. What snack felt like the best treat in the world?
  5. What would little me want to do this weekend?
  6. When did I stop drawing, dancing, singing or playing pretend? Do I know why?
  7. What toy would I buy for my younger self today?
  8. Plan one hour of play for this week with no goal at all.
  9. What makes me feel like a kid now, in a good way?
  10. What outdoor place felt magic to me as a child?
  11. Which childhood friend do I think about, and what did we do together?

Inner child journal prompts about what you needed

Take these slowly. You can answer one and come back to the rest another day.

  1. What did I need more of as a child: attention, praise, quiet, safety or fun?
  2. When did I feel unseen as a kid?
  3. What did I wish an adult would say to me back then?
  4. What was I afraid of as a child, and who comforted me, if anyone?
  5. How was I treated when I made mistakes?
  6. What feelings was I allowed to show at home? Which ones did I hide?
  7. Did I have to grow up fast in any way? How?
  8. What did I do to get love or approval?
  9. Who made me feel safe, and what did they do?
  10. What did I want to ask for but never did?
  11. What is one moment from childhood that still feels tender to think about?

Prompts about the messages you learned

  1. What did I learn about crying when I was young?
  2. What words were used to describe me as a child? Do I still believe them?
  3. What did I learn about being good?
  4. What did my family say or show about people who rest?
  5. What did I learn about anger by watching the adults around me?
  6. What rule from childhood do I still follow without thinking?
  7. Which childhood belief helps me now, and which one hurts?
  8. What did I learn about asking for help?
  9. How was love shown in my home, and how do I show it now?
  10. What would I like to unlearn?

Letters to your younger self

Letters are one of the most gentle ways to do inner child work. Write as the caring adult you are now.

  1. Write a letter to myself at age five. What does that child need to hear?
  2. Write to my ten year old self about the day they felt most alone.
  3. Tell my younger self one thing that turned out better than they feared.
  4. Write a letter from my younger self to me now. What do they ask?
  5. Write a thank you to my younger self for getting me here.
  6. Tell my teenage self what I wish they had known about friends.
  7. Write a promise to my inner child that I can keep this month.
  8. Write a sorry note to my younger self for the times I have been hard on them as an adult.
  9. Write a bedtime story for little me.

Prompts for caring for your inner child now

Some people call this reparenting: giving yourself today the care you needed then.

  1. When I feel small or scared today, what does that part of me need?
  2. What is one thing I can give myself now that I did not get then?
  3. How do I talk to myself when I make a mistake? How would a loving parent talk?
  4. What reaction of mine feels bigger than the moment? How old do I feel when it happens?
  5. What does my inner child need when I am tired?
  6. What would a safe grown-up do for me today?
  7. Which person in my life now makes my inner child feel safe?
  8. What is a need I usually ignore until it becomes urgent?
  9. What would I let little me do that I do not let myself do now?
  10. What does comfort look like for me as an adult?
  11. Write a list of feelings my inner child is allowed to have.
  12. What part of my childhood am I ready to honor, not just heal?

Tips for gentle inner child work

  • Balance hard with happy. For every heavy prompt, answer a joy prompt too.
  • Keep sessions short. Ten to fifteen minutes is enough. You do not have to process your whole childhood at once.
  • Notice your body. If your chest tightens or you feel shaky, stop, breathe and do something soothing.
  • Pair with self-kindness. Our self-love journal prompts and healing journal prompts are good companions.
  • Get support when you need it. Bringing your journal to a therapist can be a helpful way to start a conversation. See therapy journal prompts.
  • Keep it private. Our free online diary keeps pages in your browser, with no account.

If you want to go deeper into hidden patterns, some people move on to shadow work journal prompts. Take your time before you do.

Questions people ask

What is inner child journaling?

Inner child journaling is writing to, about or from the child you used to be. It helps you remember what you loved, notice what you needed and did not get, and offer yourself kindness now. Many people use letters to their younger self, memory prompts and questions about old beliefs.

How do I start inner child work in a journal?

Start with happy memories, like your favorite toy, game or place as a child. Look at an old photo if you have one. Once you feel connected, try writing a short, kind letter to your younger self. Go slowly, and leave harder prompts for when you feel steady.

Can inner child journaling be harmful?

It can bring up painful memories, especially if your childhood was hard. That is why it helps to go slowly, start with joyful prompts and stop when something feels too heavy. If memories feel overwhelming or keep you from sleeping or working, talk to a licensed therapist. In a crisis, contact local emergency services, or call or text 988 in the US.

What do I say to my inner child?

Say what you needed to hear back then. Many people write things like: You are safe now. It was not your fault. You are allowed to have feelings. I am proud of you. I will take care of us. Speak gently, the way you would to any small child.

How often should I do inner child journaling?

Once or twice a week is plenty for most people. This kind of writing can be tiring, so give yourself time between sessions. Some people do a short play prompt more often, because adding small moments of joy is part of the work too.
