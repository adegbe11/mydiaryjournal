75 journal prompts for anxiety

Journal prompts for anxiety help you move worries out of your head and onto the page, where they are easier to look at. Below are 75 calm, practical prompts for anxious moments, racing thoughts, social worry and bedtime, plus an anxiety journal example you can copy. Write in the private diary at the top of this page.

How to use anxiety journal prompts

Anxiety likes to stay vague. A feeling of dread with no edges is harder to handle than a clear sentence on a page. Writing gives the worry a shape, and once it has a shape you can decide what to do with it.

  1. Name it.Write the worry in one plain sentence. I am worried that...
  2. Notice your body.Where do you feel it? Tight chest, busy stomach, tense jaw. Write that down too.
  3. Pick one prompt.Use the list below that matches your moment: in the middle of anxiety, racing thoughts, social worry or night time.
  4. End with a next step.One small action, or permission to leave it for now.

Journaling can help with everyday worry, but it is not a replacement for professional care. If anxiety is getting in the way of your life, talk to a doctor or counselor. If you are in crisis, contact local emergency services. In the US, call or text 988.

Prompts for when anxiety is high right now

Keep these very short. The goal is to slow down, not to solve everything.

  1. Name five things I can see, four I can hear and three I can touch right now.
  2. Breathe in for four counts and out for six, three times. How does my body feel now compared to before?
  3. What exactly am I afraid will happen in the next hour?
  4. Is this feeling about something happening now, or something that might happen later?
  5. What is one thing in this room that feels steady or comforting?
  6. Rate this anxiety from one to ten. I will check the number again in fifteen minutes.
  7. What would I need to hear right now from someone who loves me?
  8. Write the worry in giant letters, then write it again tiny. Which one feels more true?
  9. What has passed before that felt just as urgent as this?
  10. What is one thing I can do with my hands for the next five minutes?

Journaling prompts for anxiety and racing thoughts

  1. Brain dump: write every thought spinning around, without sorting it.
  2. Which of these thoughts showed up more than once today?
  3. What is the worst case, the best case and the most likely case?
  4. If the worst case happened, how would I cope? Who would help?
  5. Am I trying to predict the future or read someone's mind? Which one?
  6. What do I actually know for sure about this situation?
  7. How much will this matter in a week? In a year?
  8. What would I tell my best friend if this were their worry?
  9. Which worry can I schedule for later? Write it a time and a date.
  10. What question am I trying to answer by worrying? Can it be answered today?
  11. Is there a worry here I have already solved but keep reopening?
  12. What does this anxiety want to protect me from?

Anxiety journal prompts about triggers and patterns

Looking back over a week or two of entries can show what tends to set off your anxiety. A mood journal can help you track it.

  1. What was happening right before my anxiety spiked today?
  2. Which times of day do I feel most anxious? Most calm?
  3. Does caffeine, sugar, skipped meals or poor sleep change how I feel?
  4. Which places, people or tasks make my chest tighten?
  5. What do I usually do when I feel anxious? Does it help in the long run?
  6. What am I avoiding because it makes me nervous? What is avoiding it costing me?
  7. When did I first remember feeling this kind of worry?
  8. What early signs tell me anxiety is building before it gets big?
  9. How does my anxiety change on weekends, holidays or vacations?
  10. What has helped me calm down in the past, even a little?

Journal prompts for social anxiety

  1. Describe an upcoming social event that worries me. What part scares me most?
  2. What do I imagine people are thinking about me? What else could they be thinking?
  3. Write about a conversation that went better than I feared.
  4. What is one small social goal I could try this week, like saying hello first?
  5. After an awkward moment, how long do I replay it? What if I let it go after one replay?
  6. Who am I most relaxed around? What do they do that helps?
  7. What would I say or do differently if I were not afraid of looking silly?
  8. What do I like about myself that comes out when I feel comfortable?
  9. Plan an easy exit line for a situation that worries me.
  10. How would I feel if a friend made the same small mistake I am worried about?

Prompts for anxiety about work, school and the future

  1. What deadline is weighing on me? Break it into three tiny steps.
  2. What is the first five-minute task I could do to start?
  3. What am I afraid will happen if I do not do this perfectly?
  4. What does good enough look like for this project?
  5. Which future what-if is taking up the most room in my head?
  6. What can I plan for, and what do I have to let unfold?
  7. Who could I ask for help or more time?
  8. What is one money worry I can look at clearly this week, with real numbers?
  9. Write about a time I was ready for something I thought I could not handle.
  10. What would I do this week if I trusted myself a bit more?

Anxiety journal prompts for bedtime

Worries often get loud at night. Writing them down can let your mind set them aside until morning. More ideas in journal prompts before bed.

  1. What is still on my mind as I get ready for bed? Park it here for tomorrow.
  2. What is one thing that went fine today, even though I worried about it?
  3. What will I do first tomorrow morning about the thing that is bothering me?
  4. What does my body need to feel ready for sleep?
  5. Describe the most restful place I can imagine in as much detail as I can.
  6. What can I forgive myself for before I sleep tonight?
  7. Which worry is a night worry that usually shrinks by morning?
  8. List three things I am looking forward to this week, however small.
  9. What would I like to dream about tonight?
  10. Write one calm sentence to say to myself if I wake up in the night.

Calming prompts to build confidence

  1. List ten hard things I have already done in my life.
  2. What is a skill or quality that helps me when things get tough?
  3. Write about a time I was brave even though I was scared.
  4. What does calm look like for me? How would I move, talk and breathe?
  5. What are three things I can say to myself when anxiety starts?
  6. What would my life look like if anxiety had a quieter voice?
  7. Who in my life believes in me? What would they remind me of?
  8. What does progress, not perfection, look like this month?
  9. What is one comfort I can always return to, like a walk or a song?
  10. Write a thank you to my body for trying so hard to keep me safe.
  11. What is one brave thing I will try this week, and how will I reward myself?
  12. What would I like to remember the next time anxiety tells me I cannot do it?
  13. What part of today proved my worry wrong?

Anxiety journal example and template

Here is what a short anxiety journal entry can look like. Notice it moves from the worry to the facts to one next step.

A simple anxiety journal template

  1. What am I worried about? (one sentence)
  2. Where do I feel it in my body?
  3. Anxiety level from one to ten
  4. What do I know for sure?
  5. Worst case, best case, most likely case
  6. One small next step
  7. Anxiety level after writing

You can fill in these seven lines in the diary above any time worry shows up. For a step by step guide, see how to journal for anxiety. If faith helps you, try our Christian journal prompts for anxiety.

Tips for journaling with anxiety

  • Write fast, not neat. The point is to get it out, not to make it pretty.
  • Set a worry window. Give yourself fifteen minutes a day to write worries. Outside that time, jot a note and save it for the window.
  • Re-rate your anxiety. Writing a number before and after shows you that feelings do change.
  • Read back weekly. You may notice that most worries never happened, which can make the next one easier to doubt.
  • Do not force deep prompts in a panic. Use the grounding prompts first, then go deeper later.
  • Pair writing with care. A walk, a glass of water or a call to a friend can follow any entry.

Questions people ask

Does journaling help with anxiety?

For many people it does. Writing worries down can make them feel smaller and more clear, and it helps you separate facts from fears. It also shows patterns over time, like which situations or habits make anxiety worse. Journaling works best alongside other support, and it is not a replacement for professional care if anxiety is strong or constant.

What do you write in an anxiety journal?

Write the worry in one sentence, how it feels in your body, how strong it is from one to ten, what you know for sure and one small next step. The template above walks you through it. On calmer days you can write about triggers, patterns and what has helped you before.

When is the best time to journal for anxiety?

Many people like writing at night so worries do not keep them awake, or in the morning to set a calm tone. You can also write in the moment when anxiety spikes. Some find a set daily worry window helps most. Try a few times and keep the one that suits you.

What if writing about my worries makes them worse?

If going over a worry makes it grow, switch to grounding prompts or write about what is going well instead. Keep entries short and always end with a next step or something calming. If writing keeps making you more anxious, talk to a doctor or counselor about other ways to cope.

Should I see a professional for anxiety?

If anxiety is affecting your sleep, work, school, relationships or daily life, it is a good idea to talk to a doctor or counselor. Anxiety is common and treatable. Your journal can help you explain what you have been feeling. If you are in crisis, contact local emergency services, or in the US, call or text 988.
