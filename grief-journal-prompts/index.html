70 grief journal prompts for loss

What is a grief journal?

A grief journal is a private place to write about someone or something you have lost. It might be a parent, a partner, a friend, a child, a pet, a pregnancy, a marriage or a way of life. There is no right way to grieve and no right way to write about it.

Many people find that writing helps when grief feels too big to say out loud. You can tell stories, write letters to the person, record the hard days and notice, slowly, the moments that feel a little lighter. A grief journal for adults does not need to be neat or brave. It just needs to be honest.

Grief is not something to fix, and journaling is not a replacement for support. A grief counselor, support group, faith leader or doctor can help, especially if you feel stuck or unable to cope. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact local emergency services right away. In the US, call or text 988.

Grief journal prompts for remembering

  1. Write about the first time I met them, or my earliest memory of them.
  2. What did their voice sound like? What words or phrases did they always use?
  3. Describe a normal day with them, from morning to night.
  4. What was their laugh like, and what made them laugh most?
  5. What is a story about them I never want to forget?
  6. What did their home, car or favorite chair smell like?
  7. What food reminds me of them?
  8. What song, show or place do I connect with them?
  9. What did they teach me without trying to?
  10. What were their small habits that I loved, or that drove me a little crazy?
  11. Describe a photo of us together and what was happening just outside the frame.
  12. What did they care about most in the world?

Prompts to write to the person you lost

Many people find comfort in writing straight to the person. Start with Dear and their name.

  1. Dear ___, here is what has happened since you have been gone.
  2. What I never got to say to you is...
  3. Thank you for...
  4. I am sorry for...
  5. I wish you could see...
  6. The thing I would ask you if you could answer is...
  7. Here is what I want you to know about how much you mattered.
  8. This week I almost called you to tell you...
  9. If I could have one more hour with you, we would...
  10. What I am doing to keep your memory close is...

Grief journal prompts for hard days

Some days the grief is loud. Keep these entries short. One line is enough.

  1. What does my grief feel like today? Describe it as a shape, a weight or a color.
  2. What moment today caught me off guard?
  3. What do I need right now: to cry, to rest, to talk, to be distracted?
  4. Who could I reach out to today, even with a short message?
  5. What is the hardest part of mornings or evenings without them?
  6. What feelings am I surprised by: anger, relief, guilt, numbness?
  7. What do people say that helps? What do they say that hurts?
  8. What is one small thing I managed to do today?
  9. What would they say to me if they saw me this sad?
  10. What comfort can I give myself tonight?

Prompts about guilt, regret and unfinished things

  1. Is there something I wish I had done differently? Write it out fully.
  2. What would I say to a friend carrying this same regret?
  3. What did I do right, in the time we had?
  4. What feels unfinished between us? How could I honor it now?
  5. Is there anger I feel guilty about? Let it have a page.
  6. What do I need to forgive myself for?
  7. What do I need to forgive them for, if anything?
  8. Which what-if keeps coming back? What is true even if I cannot answer it?

Grief journal prompts for anniversaries and holidays

  1. How do I want to spend their birthday or the anniversary this year?
  2. Which holiday tradition do I want to keep, change or skip for now?
  3. What could I do to include their memory at a family gathering?
  4. What do I dread about the coming season? What would make it gentler?
  5. Who else is missing them on this day? Could we reach out to each other?
  6. What would they have wanted me to do on this day?
  7. Write about a favorite holiday memory with them.
  8. What is one new small ritual I could start in their honor?

Grief journal ideas for carrying them forward

Grief does not end, but it can change shape. In time, many people find ways to carry their love forward. Our healing journal prompts may help when you are ready.

  1. What part of them do I see in myself?
  2. What lesson from them do I want to pass on?
  3. How has losing them changed what matters to me?
  4. What is one way I can honor them this month?
  5. Who has shown up for me during this grief? How can I thank them?
  6. What moments have felt a little lighter lately?
  7. Is it okay to laugh and enjoy things again? What would they say?
  8. What do I want my life to look like a year from now?
  9. What would I like to tell someone who has just lost someone?
  10. What new thing have I learned about myself through grieving?
  11. How can I keep talking about them with people who knew them?
  12. Make a list of things I want to do that they would have loved.
  13. What does it mean to me to keep someone in my heart?
  14. What has grief taught me about love?
  15. Which memory brings a smile now instead of only tears?
  16. Where do I feel close to them?
  17. What is one thing I would like to make, plant or create in their memory?
  18. How do I want to remember them ten years from now?
  19. What do I want to say to them today, just today?
  20. What would I like to write here again next month?
  21. What small sign of life going on did I notice this week?
  22. How has my grief changed since the first week? What has stayed the same?

Tips for keeping a grief journal

  • There are no rules. Write every day or once a month. Write a page or one word.
  • Grief is not in stages you must follow. Feelings come in waves and in any order. Your journal can hold all of them.
  • Save small things. Write down their sayings, recipes, jokes and stories before they fade. Those pages become a treasure.
  • Be gentle on hard dates. Plan a short entry, then do something kind for yourself.
  • Keep it private. This diary is saved only in your browser on this device. Print it or save it as a PDF if you want to keep it forever.
  • Find support. Talking to people who knew them, a support group or a counselor can help in ways writing cannot.

Questions people ask

What is a grief journal?

A grief journal is a private notebook or diary where you write about a loss. You might record memories, write letters to the person who died, describe how you feel each day, or plan how to get through anniversaries. It gives your grief a safe place to go when it is too hard to talk about.

What should I write in a grief journal?

Write whatever is true for you: stories and memories, things you wish you had said, how today felt, what you miss, and what helps. Letters to the person are very common. The prompts above give you a place to start when you cannot find the words yourself.

Does journaling help with grief?

Many people find that writing helps them express feelings that are hard to say out loud and keeps memories safe. It will not take grief away, and it is not meant to. It works best alongside support from people you trust, and from a grief counselor or doctor if you feel stuck.

How soon after a loss should I start a grief journal?

Whenever you feel ready. Some people write in the first days, others wait months. Early on, very short entries are fine, like one memory or one feeling. There is no deadline. You can also start a journal years after a loss, especially around anniversaries.

Is a grief journal only for the death of a person?

No. You can grieve a pet, a pregnancy, a friendship, a marriage, a home, your health or a future you hoped for. Any loss that matters to you is worth writing about. Adjust the prompts above to fit what you are missing.
